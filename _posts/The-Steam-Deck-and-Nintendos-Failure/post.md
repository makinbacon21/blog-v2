In the past month, we’ve seen two different handheld console announcements from two very different companies, and what they presented us with was both expected and representative of larger problems.

![Nintendo Switch](/post/The-Steam-Deck-and-Nintendos-Failure/oled.webp)

## The Nintendo Switch
Nintendo, about four years after the launch of the original Switch handheld, announced the new Nintendo Switch OLED Model—the same exact console with a better screen, stand, and dock, in addition to more internal storage. When the Switch was released back in 2017, it was a mobile powerhouse, boasting a custom revision of Nvidia’s Tegra X1 SOC (System-on-a-chip), with its custom Maxwell-architecture GPU. The chip, released in 2015, outpaced most mobile chips in graphical performance, although its big.LITTLE 4/4 core design left something to be desired in CPU performance. The Switch, originally slated to have 2GB of RAM, ended up with 4GB thanks to Capcom’s intercession, which was sufficient back in 2017 but later in the Switch’s lifecycle filled up quickly. This original model, codenamed `Icosa`, used the `Erista` T210 chip and was a pretty great device, with a wide selection of games and much untapped potential. Once an easy software exploit was discovered, it’s homebrew community thrived, porting games, operating systems, and other open-source software to the Switch. Some later models contained a minor revision blocking the exploit, but the Switch continued to thrive. Back in 2019, the hardware was slightly refreshed with a new, more power-efficient chip—this model was dubbed `Iowa`, and used the `Mariko` T214 chip variant, which by nature cannot be exploited by the same software hack. The Switch Lite, dubbed `Hoag`, also used the T214 chip, and featured a PSP-like design with permanently attached joycons and no dock support. This model added colors and provided a different experience from the stock Switch, but overall the hardware remained largely untouched aside from some tiny quality-of-life changes. As the new Xbox Series X/S and PS5 launched, people began to hope for a new Switch revision. After all, the Switch was from 2017 and still used a somewhat outdated mobile processor that had been superseded by both new X1 revisions and whole new architectures. This led to the popular belief that Nintendo was going to release a brand new Switch, with a new custom chip based on Nvidia’s new Ampere architecture. Some popular “leakers” corroborated this with some supposedly leaked PCB designs. However, as anyone in the Switch homebrew community will tell you, there was never a shot at anything but what we got—a disappointing, mostly visual, minor update disguising an aging console unable to even dream of keeping up with the rest of the industry. The few ports it receives stutter and often run at unacceptable framerates, and others have even turned to cloud gaming…still at full retail price. The Switch’s homebrew scene keeps original models active and often at insane prices on eBay, but newer models are quickly losing their lives with the only real reasons to get a Switch being 1st party titles, the numbers of which are dwindling this late in the console’s life cycle. So why release this variant? The hardware is virtually identical, with the same Mariko hardware and only a new codename to differentiate the device—`Aula`. The internal storage might be bigger, but it’s not enough to avoid needing an SD card for anyone with a bunch of games. The console isn’t exploitable, at least without a modchip that doesn't exist and never will in the mainstream, the screen is the same less-than-a-cellphone resolution, the JoyCons are still crappy, and no RAM was added. Had RAM been added, some more games could have been ported, better new games could have been developed, and the life cycle would have been extended…but unfortunately this was not the case. The community has expressed their profound disappointment, and developers seem to feel the same way. After all, it was Capcom that was demanding 4GB of ram back in 2017 to cram a game in, and titles four years later are even more demanding. I think the developer response is best summed up by Modern Vintage Gamer in [THIS](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XYJOJj40ONQ) video, where he speaks on his own Switch development experience and how he feels about the OLED model.

## The Steam Deck
Rumors of a Steam handheld console had been circulating for a while when the announcement dropped for the Steam Deck--a Switch Lite-esque handheld console, but with specs much more on par with a low-budget modern PC. Compared to the Switch (OLED Model), the Deck has a more ergonomic controller design, with more buttons available for use, albeit without the ability to detach. Both consoles have 7" touchscreens, side-attached controller halves, a headphone jack, and a microSD card slot for expandable storage, but the Deck pulls ahead with more, faster storage on the two more expensive models. The Deck's resolution is also a bit higher, at 800p instead of 720p, with a 16:10 aspect ratio. The biggest advantage aside from specs is game and software availability. The device runs Linux, allowing the user to run any Linux apps, and reportedly supports at least the majority of the Steam library via Valve's Proton Linux-Windows compatability layer. Plus, the system is x86_64-based, allowing it to run most industry-standard programs without needing an emulation layer or a recompilation. The device should be fairly easy to tweak for booting other OS's, but there shouldn't be much need for this given the preinstalled Arch Linux-based SteamOS 3.0. For any games unable to run, game streaming should be a breeze, as most game streaming clients have binaries for Arch Linux. The Deck also sports a Bluetooth 5.0 adapter, which supports BT audio natively, can theoretically use any USB-C display/display adapter, and requires no subscription for online service--all massive advantages over the Switch. The final blow to Nintendo is the price--the cheapest model is $399, only $50 more than the Switch OLED Model, and with all the above advantages.

## Conclusion
Almost any way you stack it, this is a massive loss for Nintendo. While of course Nintendo's exclusives and detachable controllers will keep it alive, the Steam Deck takes the form factor and makes it what it should have been all along--a full computer, with ergonomic controllers and universal software support. Nintendo's lack of innovation over the past four years, coupled with its relatively miniscule software catalog, has brought it to its knees. Had they launched a brand new model with a new chip design (ignoring such technical difficulties as shader compatability for argument's sake) to allow the Switch to run a new generation of games, they might have taken the victory here. Steam definitely has a much larger game catalog, and Arch Linux itself supports a massive amount of software, but Nintendo's exclusives and simple design (and lower price if it were the same as the OLED model) would have brought it above Valve's offering. Taking advantage of Nvidia's DLSS technology specifically could have allowed the Switch to perform even better than the Deck, while consuming far less battery power thanks to the far more efficent ARM architecture. Had the Switch OLED model just bumped the handhelp res to 1080p, which many games would have no trouble with, there would have been a lot less of a problem. But now, Nintendo faces the same reality it faced with the Wii U--some great first party games, but a recycled console design that fails to effectively compete with or stand out from the rest of the industry.

## TL;DR
Bad news for Nintendo.
