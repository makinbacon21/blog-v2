This is a very very very late post. August 3rd marked the final day of the long awaited first flight of *Halo Infinite*. The short, closed-access, multiplayer sample gave players and stream watchers a look at *Infinite*'s multiplayer experience and gameplay. I was lucky enough to be invited to the first flight along with famed streamers like [moistcr1tikal](https://www.twitch.tv/moistcr1tikal) and *Halo* YouTubers like [UberNick](https://www.youtube.com/user/UberNickYT), and I had a great time. Let's jump into my thoughts, shall we?

## Gameplay and Sandbox

The tech test revealed most about *Infinite*'s gameplay. I (and pretty much every other *Halo* fan) was pretty concerned about whether *Infinite*'s would satisfy both fans' craving for classic *Halo* style gameplay and more modern trends towards faster-paced combat and movement. However, these fears were quickly assuaged. After loading into my first Bot Slayer match, I was able to quickly pick up the mechanics with some quick control modifications, and there was a lot to like. As alluded to in trailers and the Multiplayer ViDoc from around E3, *Infinite* returns to the sandbox-based gameplay style of earlier games. *4* and *5* attached more mechanics to the player, often doing so poorly, which felt a lot more like modern *Call of Duty* games and less like *Halo*. For those who don't know, a sandbox-driven game is one centered around items and environments present on the map that are usable by the player, generally in a way that provides a diverse set of options for how to approach different situations. For example, in *Halo 3*, one could grab an Active Camo power-up to creep up on other players, or activate a map element like the bridge on the Last Resort map (Zanzibar from *2*) to grab an Energy Sword, but doing so would put the player out in the open as they ran along the bridge. One might open the gate on High Ground to allow vehicles in, or maybe leave it closed as to prevent the enemy from ramming a Ghost right down their team's throat. *Infinite* feels very similar. There are many ways to approach each conflict, with certain points a team may want to control, power weapon spawns at key locations, carry-able power-ups like Overshield and Active Camo, and of course the new Grappleshot, which when used correctly can yield amazing gameplay moments. Grappling up to a ledge while swinging around the corner of a building on Bazaar grants you a perfect opportunity to blast your opponent with your Bulldog...or for them to hit you from around the corner with a well placed ricocheting shot from their Heatwave.

Each gun feels a bit overpowered...if you know how to use it. In *4*, most guns felt rather underpowered, with some power weapons that just dominated games and led to all sorts of gameplay imbalances. *5* made it even worse--each gun had a million variants, and some were good and some just weren't. *Infinite* boasts a colorful set of guns, each serving a unique role in the sandbox. Guns like the Pulse Carbine aren't hard to master, but players could get kills more efficiently if they take the time to learn a gun like the Heatwave. The Heatwave is probably the most interesting gun in the roster, but requires high levels of precision to kill quickly--if you don't have this, the time-to-kill makes picking it up a bit of a waste. The hardest gun in the roster is definitely the Skewer--a one-hit-kill harpoon gun with a slow projectile velocity, significant bullet drop, and an insane reload time. As a matter of fact, the reload time is about as long as it takes a skilled player to get a kill with a basic gun like the MA40 Assault Rifle. One common criticism brought up by classic *Halo* fanatics is the inclusion of Sprint and Clamber, and I get where they come from, but I personally like what they did with it here. Sprint is barely faster than standard walk speed, and mostly serves to give players an inertial boost for jumps, etc., and Clamber just feels natural in the environments, especially with the Grappleshot. The other Spartan Abilities were (rightfully) killed off, and I do not miss them at all, but these two feel essential for the modernization of *Halo*. I've actually grown a bit irritated at some *Halo* content creators like [Favyn](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCbJl2-d_5C3br6XA_rayc3A) for their insistence that Sprint *must* hurt the game. It serves a fundamentally different purpose in *Infinite*, and it's pretty optional for most situations in the game. I can appreciate the viewpoint that Sprint is not great in *Infinite*, but many creators called out the inclusion of Sprint as a really bad thing *before even playing the game*. It often feels a lot closer to the creators refusing to accept deviations from the classic formula and looking only for a nostalgia-fueled fan game rather than suggesting that it isn't right in the context of *this* game. Don't get me wrong--I think Favyn is probably the content creator who understands *Halo*'s struggles the most (see his videos about *Halo*'s struggle to evolve), but I think that while he is definitely right that attaching new mechanics to the player isn't the right way for *Halo* to evolve, these two remaining abilities serve to help the player better access the sandbox of the game. A few times I thought to myself, "Why did they even include Sprint in here? It's not much faster and can't possibly add that much." Then I made some jumps to grab the Gravity Hammer on Recharge, leapt between buildings on Bazaar, and got destroyed for my faster movement despite my Active Camo, and I began to see the bigger picture. The whole picture became clear to me when I hopped back in some *Halo 3* matches in *The Master Chief Collection* after the tech test ended. It just felt so...slow. Every moment of *Infinite* was exciting and different, and while I still love the classic experience, *Infinite* feels like the natural modern evolution of *Halo 3*. From sandbox, to slightly upgraded mechanics, to style of gameplay, everything feels like a newer, faster, possibly more fun version of *3*'s multiplayer, leaving me to wonder if my attachment to *3*'s multiplayer may have just been nostalgia--I invite others who've tried the tech test to ponder the same question. All in all, the gameplay (to me at least) feels tight, fun, fast, and absolutely like *Halo*.

## Technical Stuff

*Infinite* may have blown me away in terms of the game design, but what about the technical aspects of it? I will preface this by saying that I've only played *Infinite* on PC, with only second-hand knowledge of what it's like on consoles. As far as I can tell, it runs like a *dream* on consoles. Scaling well between the Xbox One and Xbox Series consoles, *Infinite* might not look as great on older machines, but it certainly runs well. I think the execution of the console graphics and performance scaling was perfect, to 343's credit. The same cannot really be said for the PC version. While the array of options available made me incredibly happy, with even per-weapon offset options and specific graphics settings to be the best for the widest array of PC gamers, there were some glaring issues. The first is one that is also present in the PC version of the Master Chief Collection, although I generally attributed that to the older games not supporting the feature, and that would be the lack of TAA (Temporal Anti-Aliasing) and other modern anti-aliasing algorithms. This shows a notable difference in quality compared to other modern shooters. Infinity Ward's *CoD: Modern Warfare (2019)* and *CoD: Warzone* are great examples--they use a new game engine as well, but one with far more graphical options that one would expect on PC, supporting even Filmic SMAA T2X, an intensive but incredible AA algorithm. Graphics aren't everything though, and this is certainly excusable--the next point is not. 343 neglected to include *exclusive fullscreen mode*. This may not mean much to you, but believe me its impact is felt every second of the game. I was trying to figure out why the framepacing was so terrible at decent resolutions, and I realized it was because the game was locked in borderless fullscreen mode. This essentially means that the fullscreen window is composited by the Desktop Window Manager--an ancient and unoptimized piece of code from Microsoft that also handles compositing the desktop and other windows. This results in a terrible experience, as the DWM is not equipped to deal with modern games at modern resolutions with the Windows 10 or 11 desktop running, along with possibly other windows in the background. Exclusive fullscreen is included in most games, going back decades, but was somehow neglected here. This is quite disappointing as it cripples performance and causes ridiculous stuttering and I can only hope it will be added before release.

## Overall Impressions

I am psyched for *Infinite*. Some recent news involving features not available at launch along with my issues with the engine give me pause, but I had a ton of fun with the tech test and it had very little content--I'm very excited to see everything else, and to play the new campaign. The lack of Forge at launch is concerning and disappointing, but I am still excited for the campaign, multiplayer, and the rest of the game.
