# Intro

This is gonna be a bit of a weirder post than usual. I've written about my food allergy issues
at school before, but the dining staff seem to be working on massive improvements. Most recently,
they asked me to make a video talking about my experiences living with food allergies to show to
(as far as I can tell) other students and staff in training. I figured I didn't want the script
to vanish, and it has some valuable info in it, so I might as well chuck it here for kicks.
Here ya go:

# Script

Hi! My name is Thomas Makin (Class of 25), and I'm a student living with severe food allergies.
While some allergies develop over time, and some of mine have changed over time, I have been
anaphylactic to several products since I was born. This condition has always been a part of my
life, and I'll likely always have to live with it. Dining Services has asked me to make a video
explaining my condition and how I deal with it on a daily basis on campus and in life, so here I
am.

Anaphylaxis is often confused with food intolerance--especially dairy allergies. A dairy allergy
is not a gastrointestinal issue or inability to break down protein, but rather a severe, life-
threatening condition where if I were to ingest more than a trivial amount of the stuff, my
windpipe would close and I would suffocate. Thankfully, we have many life-saving drugs like
Benadryl and Epi-Pens these days, but usage of these medications can have side effects of their
own. Benadryl can knock someone like me out for a while, leaving me unable to attend class or
think properly. Plus, overuse of antihistamines can lead to withdrawal symptoms when not in use.
Epi-Pens are even more severe--they contain essentially raw adrenaline to counteract the dilation
of the blood vessels (vasodilation) caused by anaphylaxis. This can cause damage to the heart if
overused or used improperly, as can the vasodilation of anaphylaxis itself.

So yea, anaphylaxis is no joke and isn't exactly something you can reliably take a pill for to
counteract. While many people with allergies learn to fend for themselves, those around them
must ensure that the environment is safe. If preparing food, make sure to wash equipment
thoroughly, use separate utensils for items that may be problematic, know your ingredients
beforehand, and make sure to ask allergic friends if they are airborne to anything before opening
items containing an allergen. My airborne allergies helpfully vanished in middle school, but for
many this is a struggle they will always live with. Be cautious and be courteous to the needs and
comforts of those with allergies.

For things students with allergies can do for themselves, I'd suggest first and foremost an
abundance of caution. Always just take a bite of a food product and wait before you have more to
prevent a more serious reaction. I've had my fair share of these at school from items that were
accidentally mislablled or that I took by mistake. Things happen, just be prepared. Another
important thing I'm sure you all know already is to always keep an Epi-Pen and/or Benadryl on you.
You don't want to get stuck without it when you need it.

On the flip side, Dining Services is working actively to improve thier methods and quality control
procedures to ensure mislabelling is kept to an absolute minimum and cross contamination is kept
completely out of the picture at the Free Zone. If you have an allergy not covered by the Free
Zone (as I do with chickpea and legumes), let the dining staff know. They can provide more info
and label items with your specific allergens. These improvements are crucial because the processes
in place since the transition to the new Dining Center have not always been perfect, and it is
first and foremost the responsibility of the school to provide a safe, properly labelled, and
comfortable environment for all students with allergies.

Thank you for listening, and if you have any questions please feel free to shoot me (or Dining
Services) an email.
