On September 22, 2021, Microsoft held one of their annual Surface device conferences. They announced several new devices--the Duo 2 with an actually viable design, the Pro 8 with a new 120Hz display with smaller bezels, and refreshes to the Pro X and Go 3, with a 120Hz display and better hardware respectively. However, none of these were the star of the show. Microsoft released a device with a brand-new form factor--a Surface Laptop whose screen had a double hinge halfway up, allowing it to be pulled from the base and pulled forward--still attached, but becoming a gaming monitor or drawing slate. Upon seeing the reveal of this "Surface Laptop Studio," I knew I had to have it. This device was exactly what I had been looking for since the inception of the Surface line--a high-quality laptop with high-quality drawing slate potential. In early October, soon after the launch, I ordered the i7/16GB/512GB model. Best Buy delivered it the next day, and I opened my new laptop, seemingly designed for my exact workflow, for the first time.

## The Chassis

The Surface Laptop Studio has a typical Surface Laptop-style body at first top-down glance. A curved-corner body with a thin screen and the best keyboard in the industry...wait what the hell is that on the bottom? Here we are presented the first big change from the typical Surface Laptop lineup. The base of the laptop uses a two-tiered structure like the PS2--the bottom tier is scaled down on a 2D XY plane, inset from the top layer. This gives you space to store your new Surface Slim Pen 2, which is held in by a much stronger magnetic grip compared to previous solutions and is wirelessly charged from the chassis. I'll talk more about the pen later, but it really is incredible and perfectly complements the hardware of the Laptop Studio itself. The two-tiered design also provides a nice spot for air from the dual cooling fans to expel air. The distance between the vents and the edge of the top tier means when your hands are on either side of the device, you're not covering the vents--a nice touch both for cooling performance and handling.

![Side View](/post/side.jpg)
Side view of chassis

Above the keyboard and under the bottom half of the dual-hinged, magnetic screen is something new--a *fabric hinge*. I wasn't quite sure what to make of this when I first heard about it from the website. Fabric? Like Alcantara? Like (God forbid) the Type Covers? I really didn't know. But after seeing, feeling, and using it in person, I can say that it is like neither of those. The fabric is a slightly rough but flexible material, and provides a scaffold for the screen's cables to hide behind. The material serves to protect the cables and facilitate the proper hinge folding behavior. The fabric actually extends to the bottom of the inside face of the chassis between the laptop hinge and the fabric hinge, providing an interesting texture and (I would assume) keeping tension on the fabric hinge.

## The Keyboard and Trackpad

The keyboard is a further refined version of the Surface Laptop keyboard--meaning I absolutely love it above all other laptop keyboards. The keys are satisfying to press, with tactile feedback and the *perfect* amount of actuation force. The keys are the perfect size for my fingers and the same goes for the keyboard as a whole--it feels perfectly natural typing on it. My last machine was a Surface Pro 7, and before that a Surface Laptop 1st gen--the refined SL keyboard on a device that can function as a Pro (the way I used it at least) is a game changer. The Pro keyboards are terrible--not out of some design flaw, but because it isn't possible to make such a portable, magnetic, soft-touch keyboard without wobble and low key travel. For what it was, the Type Cover was great--tactile, portable, comfortable for quick typing...but I'll take a full phat laptop keyboard over that any day. Especially this keyboard. The contrast is beyond night and day--this is a whole other universe.

![Laptop Mode](/post/laptop.jpg)
Laptop mode

The trackpad continues this--while I've hated Apple's Force Touch haptic trackpads, and expected to have issues with Microsoft's attempt at a haptic trackpad, I have had no such issues. On top of the right-click actually functioning (unlike Apple's weirdness with right-clicks/double-clicks), clicking in general feels very responsive and tactile. Plus, the OS allows customization of the haptic feedback level, letting you tune the feel of your trackpad to your liking. I personally needed a little more than the default feedback level, so I set the slider at around 75% (it unfortunately does not adjust continuously--it jumps in increments of 25%). I tried setting it at 0% just for kicks...absolutely crazy. It amazes me how haptics can so perfectly replace physical movement. The trackpad is also the perfect size, with the perfect dimensions for the screen it controls the pointer of. Speaking of the screen, let's move on to the main attraction of the Laptop Studio.

## The Screen

The Surface Laptop Studio's main selling point is its 2-in-1 screen design. With the most advanced Surface mobile screen yet, the Surface Laptop Studio boasts a 120Hz refresh rate in an ultra-thin casing, flippable on a horizontal axis in the center. The standard 3:2 Surface Laptop aspect ratio shines here--a ton of vertical space in a very manageable form factor. The odd horizontal distribution does make side-by-side editing in programs like VS Code a little odd, but it's more than manageable and can be mitigated by resizing and moving UX elements. Sliding the screen down can be a little awkward the first few times you do it--the center hinge is free-moving unlike Apple's Magic Keyboard for iPad and makes adjustment...an adjustment. I got used to it quite quickly and overall it's a lot faster and less distracting than flipping or folding a 2-in-1, and gives a slight angle for optimal drawing in tablet form. The easel form is a bit pointless--the angle is too large for eye-level content consumption, although it does seem to improve low-frequency speaker quality (more on this later).

Many instantly attacked the screen's rounded corners as stupid-looking and intrusive, not to mention unnecessary, but I disagree with this. Especially after Apple's ridiculous unveiling of their massive MacBook Pro notches, cutting bits out of the display isn't necessarily bad as long as it doesn't affect usability and looks good. The MacBooks look stupid, the top bezel increases by a large factor whenever an app is full-screened, and only two corners are round. The Laptop Studio, however, has a uniform aesthetic corner rounding clearly meant to match Windows 11. The device and software feel close, like they were designed together...except they clearly weren't. There is one big annoying issue with the rounded corners--Windows 11 has no code for it. When 11 cuts the corners of windows, it shifts title bar margins in and cuts nothing off. Window borders are rounded, logos are shifted in, and shell elements are adapted to match. However, since 11 is meant for squared-off screens, fullscreen and snapped apps have *none of this*.

![Arduino Window Corner](/post/arduino_window_corner.jpg)
![Arduino Fullscreen Corner](/post/arduino_fullscreen_corner.jpg)
Arduino IDE window vs. fullscreen comparison

You'd expect a similar logic to kick in, like Apple's obscuring of the top of the screen while the notch would be blocking things. A crude solution on Apple's part, but at least the software understands what's going on. This is not true for Windows 11, which has no idea the screen is rounded and does not shift anything, resulting in some title bar and border UI issues that hurt the overall consistency of the experience and just look stupid. They really could have fixed this quite easily--just apply the same shifting logic or enlarge the title bars. It's a 3:2 aspect ratio--it's not like that would matter too much. It's quite unfortunate because for apps like Word with larger title bars and more spacing, the corners look great--like they're meant to be there and fade into the UI seamlessly. But then you open apps like Arduino IDE or even *File Explorer* and BAM your immersion is ruined. Edge works great maximized, but when you snap it side-by-side the window border reappears and gets CUT OFF by the corners of the screen. In any case, it's not a deal-breaker for me, and I do appreciate the aesthetic value. I can only hope Microsoft makes some quick changes to the OS to fix this.

## The Speakers

The speakers for the SLS are...interesting. They're supposedly Dolby Atmos-certified for some reason, but they're still embedded in the chassis with no MacBook-esque speaker holes. I'm still unsure what to make of these--sometimes they're incredible, and sometimes they are absolutely abysmal. The first point is the tinniness--as expected, the speakers aren't incredible at playing lower frequencies. This is a bit better in easel mode, as I talked about before, but still not the world's best. Better than I've heard before or expect from laptop speakers, but that's not a high bar. The second and more important point is this weirdness I've noticed in the audio--it almost sounds like it's cutting in and out. As I've listened to very full, loud, or constant sounds, it almost sounds like it's rapidly going in and out. It's not super noticeable, but I have noticed it. When watching movies, the speakers are awesome...until the movie gets to some intense part of the soundtrack or sustained explosion sound, when the bug reintroduces itself. It's like an emulator running on a not-quite-capable machine. Very weird. Again, not game-breaking for me, but something Microsoft should look at before the next iteration of this form factor.

## A Couple More Things

There are a couple other things to talk about before ending this review. The first is some praise for the camera module. Microsoft was able to cram a 1080p front-facing webcam, complete with the IR module required for blazing-fast Windows Hello facial recognition, into the top bezel of the screen. No notch, no uneven bezels, but still feature-complete. I will say it's not quite as good as my old Surface Pro 7's webcam--2.1 megapixels vs. 5, I believe--but it gets the job done.

One other complaint I had was about the rubber treads. When I first started using the SLS, it was sliding all over the place. Having used a Surface Laptop (1st generation) for quite some time, I was surprised--Surface devices usually have great rubber feet. After a month of usage, it seems to be working a bit better, but why was it so poor out of the box? Additionally, while it does seem better, it's not perfect--it still seems fairly slippery for a modern laptop. Why swap the feet for giant treads if it's not going to work any better? Just to hide the repair screws? Seriously? This is an annoying design flaw that seems easy enough to fix.

## Ideas for Second Gen

The SLS, for its issues, is still a great device--it just needs some tweaks to make it perfect. The first change I'd make would be to implement the removable deck from the Surface Laptop 4. The top could be removed on that device to upgrade storage or repair broken parts. The SLS doesn't have this and it really sucks--to get inside, you need to remove the rubber treads and open it up, causing permanent damage. I'd love to be able to upgrade my SSD or RAM, and this would also put Microsoft solidly ahead of Apple with their refusal to make MacBooks at all repairable.

My next change would be to include magnets throughout the sides of the deck so the screen can be pulled forward smoothly and positioned both freely and securely. Right now, it has 3 settings: laptop, easel, and studio. This could be expanded so users could position based on preference and seating height. Alternatively, a Magic Keyboard-style dual rigid hinge could be implemented for a similar effect.

## My Conclusion

All in all, I really do love this device, but can't really recommend it to those who aren't willing to pay the early adopter tax. This feels like the new standard for Windows laptops on the surface (haha), with a clean aesthetic, amazing keyboard, beautiful screen, and the awesome touch/pen experience expected from the Surface line. However, a few weird bugs, software inconsistencies, and physical flaws make the Surface Laptop Studio somewhat of a polished prototype. With its excessive price and issues, it's probably not worth it for most consumers until the next iteration of this perfect form factor.
