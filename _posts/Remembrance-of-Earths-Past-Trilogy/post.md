I’ve been getting back into reading thanks to my many long days at work, and one of the first series in my backlog of books I’ve been putting off was the *Remembrance of Earth’s Past* trilogy—Cixin Liu’s sci-fi series beginning with *The Three-Body Problem*. I thoroughly enjoyed the series, which left me with few criticisms but a whole lot of real-life questions about morality, physics, and the reality we live in, which is a little scary but fuels great discussions with intellectuals. Anyway, the whole thing is a bit of a crazy ride, so let’s dive in:

## *The Three-Body Problem*
This first entry in the series begins with a flashback to China’s Cultural Revolution, introducing the novel’s grounded-in-history approach to science-fiction with a bloody scene representative of many at the time, but simultaneously giving a background for one of the main characters. Reading from an anglophonic, American born-and-raised perspective, the China-centric setting of the book was refreshing and quite interesting, as they have their own history which greatly influences the development of the story. As the plot progresses, the reader learns of this vast conspiracy hiding in the background from the perspective of an outsider, but with just enough background knowledge from chapters in other characters’ perspectives to create a bit of dramatic irony. This irony is far from frustrating, however, and generally serves to create some profound “OH SH*T” moments when the reader realizes what’s going on. The book ends with a clear necessity for a sequel, as the story is far from complete. I liked the ending from a literary standpoint, as in I felt it closed the book well and was well-written, but the story definitely needed to be completed. I’d say this was my favorite of the three books—there was constant action, intrigue, and complex physics that was for the most part grounded in real theory. My personal favorite piece of said theory was the idea of the dimensionality of fundamental particles—in one scene, the Trisolarans (the hostile alien race) unfold a proton (theorized as a higher-dimensional particle we can only see some of) into two dimensions to etch a computer processor onto it. While there are many competing theories for particle physics, especially the dimensionality of fundamental particles (string theory), I personally think this explanation is rather interesting. However, I think the possibility of etching circuits, even via nuclear forces, on a purely 2D surface is a bit unrealistic. Another of my favorite scenes was when some players of the "Three Body" game (a simulation built by an organization promoting a takeover by Trisolaris to recruit new members) built a standard Von Neumann-architecture computer using PEOPLE as logic gates. All in all, the book effectively combined complex theoretical physics with Chinese historical fiction and a hostile alien presence, along with a healthy dose of human morality and sociology. I cannot possibly recommend this book any more, and anyone with enough IQ to enjoy pondering some of the questions the book poses should pick it up today.

## *The Dark Forest*
The second book in the series picks up right around where the first left off, with the government now looking for ways to combat the impending invasion from Trisolaris. After learning the Trisolarans have left some of their proton-based sophon computers on Earth to disrupt any fundamental theory progress, spy on any attempt to stop the invasion, and communicate with their agents in the ETO (Earth-Trisolaris Organization), the newly formed PDC (Planetary Defense Council) appoints several “Wallfacers” to come up with insane plans and use practically infinite resources, all without revealing their plans by word of mouth or recording. Most of the story is told from the third-person limited perspective of Wallfacer Luo Ji, who has no plan and no clue why he was selected aside from a cryptic clue from former ETO leader Ye Wenjie relating to “cosmic sociology,” which Luo Ji eventually realizes (spoiler alert) is in fact the Dark Forest Theory solution to the Fermi paradox. This theory posits that the universe is a “dark forest” of many civilizations, all at odds with each other out of mutual fear and ignorance. This eternal stalemate is balanced until someone disturbs it. I find this theory to be incredibly interesting, and since it is the foundation of this book and the next, it gives the reader (and Luo Ji) much opportunity to think about the implications. The end of the book is probably the most satisfying of the series, and could easily have been the end of the whole thing. It felt satisfying, and wrapped up the events of the first two books quite nicely. Overall, my only problem with the book was its over-detailed nature for events that did not make use of Chekhov’s Gun—entire pieces of the book that were never really brought up again. However, this was a bit of a double-edged sword, as it also meant the elements using the literary technique were hard to predict, and it keeps the reader guessing throughout the book. Ultimately, *The Dark Forest* was very enjoyable, but definitely had spots where it picked up and slowed down, especially during Luo Ji’s reintroduction to society after hibernation. I still definitely recommend it for anyone who’s read the first book, and it had some of the best scenes in the series, but it wasn’t quite as incredible as the original.

## Death’s End
The final book in the trilogy was a bit of a clusterf*ck. It felt like a lot of the fundamental  theory it references quite often—it took place before, during, and after the events of the last book, and I can vouch that the reader’s relative time elapsed is quite long. This book expands on many parts of the previous book but explores (and sometimes totally and grossly violates) human morality a lot more. The story is mostly told from the third-person limited perspective of Cheng Xin, along with snippets from her memoir and some segments from other characters. The most compelling is probably Yun Tianming, but the most human is definitely Cheng Xin. With judgement and morals so conflicting they can only be human, Cheng manages to screw everything up more than once, but justifiably so. As far as the setting goes, the plentiful hibernation sequences create an inconsistent setting that jumps around a bit too much. It really does feel like “death’s end”—Cheng spends thousands of years in hibernation just to end up making an interesting decision at the end of time. The book continues to add detailed theoretical physics, but the theories become less and less realistic as the novel continues on, and a lot of it just feels linked unnecessary side information crammed in just because the author can. I found it very interesting and thought provoking in some odd ways, but it failed to totally satisfy me, especially with its strange and inconclusive ending. While I’d recommend it to those who read the previous books, it fails to totally live up to its predecessors, and it’s jumpy nature and excessive length leave something to be desired. It does, however, encourage some interesting inner dialogue on what it means to be human, which is a pretty cool feeling for anyone who likes philosophy.

## Conclusion
I’ve been reading science fiction for quite a long time, and the *Remembrance of Earth’s Past* trilogy is one of the best works of science fiction I’ve read. While it certainly has its pitfalls, its utilization of ultra-realistic theoretical physics, questioning of human morality, and criticisms of human society create a unique feel for a sci-fi series, and immerse the reader in a world that often feels all too real. I highly recommend the series for anyone who enjoys a good discussion on theoretical physics, or even just anyone who likes a good sci-fi book.
